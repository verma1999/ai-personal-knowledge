Franz Kafka was a prominent German-speaking Bohemian novelist and short-story writer widely regarded as one of the major figures of 20th-century literature. Born in Prague in 1883, he grew up in a middle-class Jewish family. He trained as a lawyer and completed his doctorate in law at the Charles-Ferdinand University of Prague. After his studies, Kafka worked for an insurance company, an occupation that left him little time for his true passion of writing.His unique literary style blends elements of realism and the fantastic. He typically features isolated protagonists facing bizarre, bureaucratic, or surreal predicaments. His work explores themes of alienation, existential anxiety, guilt, and absurdity. The term "Kafkaesque" was coined to describe situations that mock the incomprehensible complexity of modern systems. Among his most famous works is the 1915 novella The Metamorphosis, in which a salesman wakes up transformed into a monstrous insect. He also wrote acclaimed novels such as The Trial and The Castle, both of which deal with oppressive and unreachable authorities.Kafka had a deeply complicated and strained relationship with his domineering father, which heavily influenced his psychological narratives. He also struggled with self-doubt and burned a large portion of his own writing during his lifetime. Before his death, he instructed his close friend and literary executor Max Brod to destroy all his remaining unpublished manuscripts. Brod famously defied these instructions and published the work instead, ensuring Kafka’s posthumous global fame. Kafka suffered from poor health for much of his life and contracted tuberculosis. He died in Austria in 1924 at the young age of 40. Today, he is celebrated for predicting the cold, impersonal nature of modern society. His influence spans across literature, philosophy, and pop culture around the world.
